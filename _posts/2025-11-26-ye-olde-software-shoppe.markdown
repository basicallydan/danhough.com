The saccharine scent of Monster Energy Drink lingered as I entered the open-plan office space. Desks cluttered with physical keyboards, screens and a curious, rounded plastic object known as a "mouse" created a hectic scene. Loose-clad people with large headphones atop their heads milled about, avoiding eye contact as I approached, and I felt like I was stepping into a time capsule.

Welcome to Silicon Hamlet, a heritage village designed and run by the Centre for Bygone Technology, where artisans and actors live and work in the manner of our forebears during the Second Dot-Com Bubble of 2010-2022.

When you open your holo-pad app store and look for a game, you are presented with infinite possibilities; if you cannot find what you want, you will probably ask the pad to generate it. Still, there is a quiet power in leaving the world of on-demand escapism and returning to the simpler time of our ancestors, when specialist workers were employed to painstakingly create these diversions for our enjoyment, and it could in some cases take hours, days or even weeks.

Scholars broadly agree that programming vanished overnight, much like parenting once sufficiently engaging tablet computers were invented. But, thanks to places like Silicon Hamlet and its volunteers, we can still learn first-hand about this fascinating practice.

I stayed with a so-called _scrum_ - believed to be a loose monastic order - of software engineers for 48 hours, observing their ancient practices and even trying my hand at a bit of "coding," and here I hope to relay my experiences and maybe even inspire you to visit Silicon Hamlet for yourself.

## The workspace

The office I shared with my new colleagues was not unlike our modern offices, but some key differences stood out. Some desks were so low they could not be used without sitting on a chair, and atop every surface were several screens, keyboards, and the aforementioned "mouse", a device originally intended to control the interface of a computer, made redundant well before my time by the brain-computer interface we all now take for granted.

Many would recognise the keyboard as an artisianal novelist's tool; but most would be surprised it once had another purpose: to create software.

"For mid-bubble engineers, their keyboard is an object of prestige and social status" according to David "Jira" LaTrobe, the Product Manager of Ye Olde Code Shoppe.

"Within a scrum, one could usually determine the hierarchy based on the keyboards of the engineers."

A trainee engineer would sport a dirty white keyboard with flat keys and an old Apple logo embossed on the underside. A so-called "mid-to-senior" engineer could be identified by their chunkier keyboard with an elaborate collection of flashing lights inside of it. And for the scrum master, it was the same white keyboard as the trainee, except that it was totally clean, and placed very carefully.

LaTrobe is not convinced they were useful. "The ancient texts write of _ceremonies_, and I think the clean white keyboards were never used for typing, but only for ceremonial purposes."

## Observing the craft

As I watched senior engineer Pavel typing out commands to implement a new mechanic for a handmade game artefact, I couldn't help but admire the sense of calm he seemed to exhibit: glazed eyes, slow breathing, only stopping to wipe sweat from his brow with tattered cloth with - again - the old Apple logo printed on it.

"According to the ancient wisdom of O'Reilly," Pavel told me, without looking away from the screen, "code must be written dry. So important was this principle that surviving texts always rendered it in capital letters: DRY."

These artisans are trained on what little information they can gather from ancient eBooks inexplicably printed on paper, whose ink is now largely faded. Apparently, this same information and more was once freely available online, but naturally is now considered Dangerous For Human Consumption. The are permitted only in strictly controlled anthropological contexts such as this.

Pavel abruptly stopped typing and stared at the screen for several minutes. I assumed something had gone wrong.

"He's thinking," a trainee whispered.

The guide, overhearing, gently corrected them, "he's installing dependencies."

## Participation

On the second day, the scrum master, Kyah "Tenex" HuntA, invited me to participate in a beginner's experience. I was provided with a "laptop" – a name she tells me is derived from an ancient misunderstanding, since engineers rarely used these devices on their laps.

She showed me a laminated worksheet headed, "THE ANCIENT ART OF CODING", featuring a helpful diagram:

> *INPUT:* Requirements
>
> *PROCESS:* Typing
>
> *OUTPUT:* Software

In the corner, a warning label read: "DO NOT FEED REQUIREMENTS TO WILDLIFE."

Kyah explained that prior to automation, programmers were responsible for producing each line themselves.

I asked what a "line" did.

With practiced smile she explained, "It is part of the code."

And what did the code do?

"It became the software."

She gestured for me to start typing. As I followed the worksheet - which contained the instructions for producing a "To-Do App" - symbols appeared on the screen before me. Some the computer seemed to accept, and changed colour. Others seemed problematic, and were underlined in red. When this happened, Kyah nodded solemnly.

"A mistake," she said. "In those days, mistakes were very common."

What happened if software was made with mistakes?

"Specialised programmers were employed to comb over the code with a keen eye to look for mistakes, then grade the resulting software."

Although no surviving records explain how the grading was performed, Kyah told me there were typically three outcomes. If software was graded as defective but recoverable, it was "decompiled" and sent back to the factory floor for repurposing.

If software was of middling quality, it was sold on a "seconds" market to poorer businesses who could make do. But lowest-grade code, riddled with errors, was simply thrown away.

She showed me an example at a nearby exhibit - some code symbols were displayed on a screen with a plaque beneath reading, "Rejected Code c. 2012 containing an "infinite loop" error. The programmer responsible is believed to have been forced to attend a retrospective."

Kyah explained that despite the wastefulness of this practice, many deviant programmers relished in the act of "deleting" code, believing simplicity and brevity were virtues in the art.

## Public interpretation

Later that afternoon, a school group arrived. The children leaned over the wrought-iron barrier as a guide explained the exhibit.

"Before generative systems," she explained, "programmers had to produce software by hand. This was a very slow and error-prone process. Thankfully, machines eventually learned to do this for them."

One child raised a hand. "What did the programmers do then?"

The guide considered the question for a moment. "Woodworking, mostly."

Behind her, Pavel continued typing.

## Return to the real world

I thanked my temporary colleagues for their help after they concluded my exit ceremony - which involved removing a "personal key" from their central system and dipping my hands in corn chip dust.

Kyah shook my hand and waved. "Your legacy will live on in the logs," she said, as Pavel awkwardly returned to his desk without so much as a nod.

A final plaque near the door summarised the exhibit:

> Ultimately, programming disappeared once machines learned to write the code themselves, just as the loom once learned to weave.

This felt conclusive. Reassuring, even.

Outside, Silicon Hamlet hummed with activity. The artisans continued their work, slowly producing expensive, handmade software for tourists to show off at dinner parties. You could feel, taste, or even smell the love that went into it.

Or maybe that was just Monster Energy Drink.
